It's been a while since I updated this notebook, I have a story to tell!

H, I and our intrepid canines took a lovely road trip out east, full of hiking,
microbreweries, and sight seeing. We had started to wind our way back home, but
wanted one more hike. We had been hiking more and more during the trip, one last
good trek to cap things off seemed like a great plan before 2 days of driving to
get the rental back and sadly go back to work.

## The plan

We rolled into
[Mt Carleton provincial park](https://www.parcsnbparks.ca/en/parks/mount-carleton-provincial-park-2020156)
on Thursday night, planned to hike the
[Mt Sagamook loop](https://www.hikingnb.ca/Trails/AppalachianRegion/MountCarletonPark/MountSagamook.html)
Friday morning and hit the road home after. Sagamook is a challenging 4 hour
hike, but we're pretty experienced hikers. Totally doable, right?

## Peaceful beginnings

We stayed at Armstrong campground, absolutely lovely spot. October is very much
off season for camping in New Brunswick, we had the whole campsite to ourselves!
We had a incredible view of Nictau lake, and got a plan for the morning. The
forecast for Friday morning was grey, with projections for rain starting around
1pm. Lots of time to get up and down the mountain as packing up a campervan is
incredibly fast, and it would be a quick 20 minute drive to the trailhead.

## It all started well

We caffinated and prepped, packing pretty light for the hike, hitting the trail
in good time. Mt Sagamook has a steaper side and a more gentle side. The general
recommendation is to hike up the steap trail and then come down the easier
decsent. It was challenging hiking, offering challenging rocky paths, steep
inclines. Beautiful forest, but real care needs to be taken on the trail. We
were trying to keep a brisk pace in the cold, damp early morning. 2/3s of the
way up, a very light snow started to fall. Not ideal but we were so close to the
top, we thought it would be better to sumit and come down the other half of the
trail.

Nearing the summit the terrain gets even more challenging, with large boulders
to wind your way up. Even our dogs, aussie shepherds who are possibly part
mountain goat, needed some help for some of it! Around this time it started to
seriously snow - our mood was shifting to unease, this was quickly turning into
a bad situation. We were quite aware of the risk at this point, an injury on the
trail would be quite serious. There was cell reception near the top, but after
about 1/2 way down I only had access to the
[spotty iPhone satellite services](https://support.apple.com/en-ca/120930).

The trees thinned out, what normally would have been an amazing panoramic view
was a strange sea of white below. Mental alarm bells were ringing quietly.

## Dude, where's my keys

It was around then when I put a layer back on, and some primal part of my brain
fired a dire warning - <span style="color: red;">**DANGER, your keys are not in
your pocket**</span>. After an extensive search of my pockets, hoodie, camera
bag, indeed, the keys had fallen out somewhere on the mountain! This was a huge
problem, the rental doesn't come with a second set, and the weather was turning
quickly. Mt Carleton park is quite remote - we had about 1 day of food for us
and a little less for the dogs. We could stay the night, but the logistics of
our situation were going to become very inconvenient quickly.

We decended the same way we came up, looking for the keys as we went. The snow
was accumulating. Getting to the car, we went back up a half of the hike again,
but were both getting really tired and the snow was at a point where we thought
it was unlikely we'd find them. Cell reception rapidly dying with the snow, I
managed to get a satellite message to a friend and my family before it totally
cut out.

Thankfully the van had a keypad, so we could get into it for food and some
respite from the snow that was falling. H stayed with the dogs in the van. The
trailhead had no cell reception, there was a visitor centre about 45 minutes
that had solid WIFI. I trekked to the centre, getting a lift from some wonderful
frenchmen 2/3s of the way there.

Waterlogged, a bit frozen, shaky and quite possibly a little at shock at this
point, I got a coffee and started to make calls. It turns out the automotive
industry has been taking the problem of auto theft a little too serious, getting
a new key for a modern vehicle is **difficult**. The best the company could do
was send the key by courier, and it would arrive in 2 to 3 **business days**. W.
T. actual. F.

Given this was on a Friday, that meant the very real possibility of us being
stuck there until the following Wednesday. I generally internalize my stress,
but at this point it's fair to say I was getting foggy. The number of
problem scenarios was high, and we only had one happy path out - **find the damn
keys and get gone**.

## Camp Nictau

While I was trying to arrange a key delivery with the rental company, a security
guard came but sadly was in a hurry. They were supposed to give me a ride back,
but left me there while I continued to sort out the key situation. Grr.

Getting the despiriting plan of a Wednesday key delivery in place, I had our
first stroke of good luck, there was a small campground near the Mt Sagamook
trailhead, with a open cabin for rent! While the campervan could easily weather
the storm that was rolling through, it had limited battery power for it's gas
heater. The cabin would feel a little less doomed than staying in a dead
campervan.

None of this felt like a great plan, but sometimes you have to push forward with
shaky ground and adjust as you go.

Around the point when I was about to gloom / grumpy hike back to the camper van
on shaky legs, an absolute saint of a maintance worker struck up a conversation
with me. He drove me back, helped us get our bedding down to the cabin, helped
me get wood, start a fire, showed me around. Absolute hero. His company and help
was truly wonderful, good people exist in this world. If you ever read this good
sir, thank you so much! I wish you good luck repairing cabins in the park!
Seriously, thank you.

## The storm that befell the Pine Cabin

We stayed at the inventively named "Pine cabin" (I'm not making this up). It was
about a 12x12 pioneer log cabin, big enough for a table, bed, and a wood stove.
It did the trick for the night as an absolutely wild wind and rain storm whipped
through. Occasionally the cabin would make a log structure noises, or something
would bump into it. We had a fresh supply of low quality pine lumber to burn. I
turned the place into a sauna for a while. A new plan was hatched; in the
morning we were hiking back up that hill and **finding those keys**.

## SUCCESS

In the morning, our luck continued, the rain storm at night had wiped out the
snow! Hiking back up the hill with determination, cold instant coffee highs
(would not recommend undermixing cold instant coffee) and uncertain legs, we
stared the search. Slowly, and methodically, we made our way up the hill. It was
a needle in a haystack search, we had about 2.5km of trail to search, and really
no indication of I had lost the keys. The problem with a search like this is the
constant fight with focus, and it is very easy to second guess how long one
should be spending at any given point.

H ended up finding the keys 1/3 of the way up. The dogs had started to play with
a stick on top of them, directing her focus to the area they had fallen. We
shouted woo so much Reggie joined in with a celebratory howl. **HECK YEAH**.

## What went well

 * no one got hurt on the mountain.
 * we found the keys.
 * my high cut boots were fantastic on the trail - avoided a number of abbrasion
   injuiries and slips.

## What could be better

 * losing the keys feels pretty sloppy, won't do that again. Gooooood grief.
 * The rental company campervan backup plans for key replacement were pretty poor.
